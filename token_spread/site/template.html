What is a GPU-hour worth in tokens?

A GPU-hour has a market rental price. It also has a value as a factory for tokens: run an open-weight model on it, sell the output at public API prices, and see what it earns. The gap between those two numbers is the token spread.

    What this page is for

    AI models are sold by the token. The chips that run them are rented by the hour, and they run on electricity. This page connects the three prices.

    Two markets have grown up side by side. AI companies and hosting providers publish what they charge for every million tokens a model reads or writes. Separately, cloud companies rent out the chips those models run on, and since October 2026 the rental price of NVIDIA's main AI chips even trades as a futures contract on CME. Underneath both sits a third price: the electricity those chips burn.

    Nobody publishes how the two fit together. If you rent a chip for an hour and use it to run a popular AI model, how much would you earn selling its output? Is that more or less than the rent?

    The answer says things a rental price alone can't: whether a newer chip is cheap or expensive for the work it does, which models are priced close to their running cost, and how much room the companies selling AI have to cut prices or make a profit.

    Everything here comes from public data, is refreshed every day, and links back to its source, so you can check any number yourself.

    Four ideas you need

    GPU-hour
    One hour of one AI chip, here NVIDIA's H100 or its newer B200, rented from a cloud provider. Today that costs .
    Electricity
    Each AI chip and its share of the server draws about 1.4–1.7 kilowatts, and the building adds cooling on top. At that costs per GPU-hour: a small slice of the rent.
    Throughput
    How many tokens one chip can produce per second. It links the two prices: tokens per hour × price per token = what a GPU-hour earns. The token spread is that amount minus the rent.

      Reading these numbers

      • Power per GPU includes its server. SemiAnalysis's "all-in" figure adds the GPU's share of host CPUs, memory and network cards to the chip itself: .
      • PUE covers the building. Cooling and power conversion add 10–60% on top. SemiAnalysis uses 1.2 for AI facilities; the Uptime Institute's 2025 survey puts the global average just above 1.5.
      • Average draw is below the rating. Where InferenceX measured it, GPUs drew of their rated power at the operating points used here.         How the spread is calculated

        Four public inputs, one multiplication chain. Each step is simple; the uncertainty lives in the inputs.

        Concepts

        Token
        The unit LLMs read and write, roughly ¾ of an English word. APIs charge per million tokens.
        Input vs output tokens
        Input (prompt) tokens are processed in parallel and are cheap to serve. Output tokens are generated one at a time and cost more, so providers price them 2–6× higher.
        Blended price
        One price per million tokens that mixes input and output prices in the proportions real traffic has. Real traffic is input-heavy: about 7:1 for people chatting and far more for coding agents.
        Open-weight model
        A model whose weights are published, so anyone can serve it and anyone can benchmark it. Closed models hide their hardware, so they are left out.
        Dense vs mixture-of-experts (MoE)
        A dense model uses all its weights for every token. An MoE model routes each token through a few "experts", so a 397B-parameter model may only use 17B per token, which makes it cheaper to run than its size suggests.
        Throughput per GPU
        Total tokens (input + output) a GPU processes per second, divided across every GPU in the serving setup.
        Speed per user (interactivity)
        Output tokens per second one user sees streaming back. Below roughly 20–30 tokens/s a chat feels slow.
        Concurrency and the operating point
        Serving more requests at once raises throughput per GPU but slows each stream. The operating point is where on that trade-off curve you run. We take the highest-throughput point that keeps each user above the speed floor.
        Utilization
        The share of paid hours spent serving at that operating point. Real fleets idle at night and keep headroom for traffic spikes.
        Precision (bf16, fp8, fp4)
        How many bits each weight uses. Fewer bits mean more throughput but can cost a little quality. B200 has hardware fp4 support; H100 does not.
        Speculative decoding (MTP)
        A small draft model or extra prediction heads guess several tokens ahead, and the main model verifies them in one pass. Common in production, and it raises throughput at a given speed per user.
        Disaggregated, multi-node serving
        Splitting prompt processing and token generation onto different GPUs, sometimes across machines. Needed for very large models such as DeepSeek-R1 on H100.
        GPU rental index
        Silicon Data publishes daily H100 (SDH100RT) and B200 (SDB200RT) rental indices, built mainly from neocloud prices (plus hyperscalers and rental platforms) and standardized for machine specs, rental terms and region. CME launched cash-settled futures on them (GPU1, GPU2) on 5 October 2026.
        Owner TCO
        What a GPU-hour costs someone who owns the hardware at hyperscaler scale: depreciation, power, hosting. SemiAnalysis estimates this for InferenceX. It is a floor under rental prices.
        Token spread and spread ratio
        Spread = implied revenue − rental, in dollars per GPU-hour. Ratio = revenue ÷ rental. A ratio of 2× means a GPU-hour earns twice its rent at list API prices.
        Breakeven throughput
        Tokens per second one GPU must produce for a model's price to cover the GPU's rent at a given utilization: rent ÷ (3,600 × utilization × price per token). For open-weight models, measured throughput ÷ breakeven throughput = spread ratio.
        Price-only model
        A model shown without a spread because its throughput isn't measured in a comparable way: closed models, and open-weight models InferenceX has only tested on agentic workloads (long, mostly cached conversations) or not at all.
        Closed vs open-weight
        Closed models (Claude, GPT, Grok, Gemini, Meta Muse) are only available through their makers' APIs and cloud partners. Their size, hardware and throughput are not published, so only their prices enter this analysis.
        kWh
        Kilowatt-hour, the unit electricity is sold in: one kilowatt drawn for one hour. An H100 with its share of the server draws about 1.4 kW.
        All-in power per GPU
        The chip's own power plus its share of the server around it: CPUs, memory, network cards. SemiAnalysis estimates 1.37 kW for an H100 and 1.71 kW for a B200 at full load.
        PUE (power usage effectiveness)
        Total building power divided by IT power. A PUE of 1.3 means every kW of servers needs 0.3 kW more for cooling and power conversion.
        Neocloud
        A company that buys GPUs and rents them out by the hour or on multi-year contracts, such as CoreWeave, Lambda or Nebius. Silicon Data's rental index tracks their prices.
        Depreciation
        Spreading a machine's purchase price over its useful life. A $56,000 B200 depreciated over 6 years costs about $1.07 per hour, rented or not.
        Cost of capital
        The interest (or return investors demand) on the money used to buy the hardware. Neoclouds borrowed at around 15% early on; in 2026 CoreWeave's secured investment-grade debt priced at 5.9% and its unsecured notes at 8.5–9.6%.
        Colocation
        Renting space, power delivery and cooling in someone else's data centre, priced per kW of capacity per month. North American wholesale space averaged $196 per kW-month in late 2025 (CBRE).
        Occupancy
        The share of hours a GPU is actually rented out. Idle hours still cost depreciation, interest and space, so profit falls fast when occupancy drops.
        EBITDA
        Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization: revenue minus cash running costs. GPU firms report high EBITDA margins because their biggest costs, hardware and interest, sit below that line.
        Breakeven utilization
        The utilization at which revenue equals rent. Above 100% means the model cannot pay its rent on that GPU at market prices, however busy it is.

        Caveats

        Gaps, uncertainties, and what would fix them

        Data gaps in this build

          Biggest uncertainties

          • Which point on the throughput curve. Dropping the speed floor can double throughput per GPU. Benchmarks use random data at fixed lengths; production traffic has prefix-cache hits, bursts and long-context tails.
          • Configurations differ across GPUs. The best published point may use speculative decoding on one GPU and not the other, or multi-node serving. Part of the B200 advantage may be better-tuned software, not silicon.
          • Stale benchmarks. Some model and GPU pairs have not been re-run for months; inference software improves fast, so their throughput is likely understated.
          • Unmeasured mixes. The base 8:1 mix is measured. 3:1 is interpolated between the measured 1:1 and 8:1 points; 10:1 is held at 8:1.
          • Cached input is not priced. Agent traffic (most tokens on OpenRouter) is around 100:1 input:output with about 90% of prompt tokens served from cache at a steep discount, and caching also makes the GPU faster. Neither effect is modelled, so revenue for agent-heavy traffic is uncertain in both directions.
          • List prices and on-demand rents. OpenRouter shows public per-token prices without volume or cache discounts. Rental is Silicon Data's standardized index, not a term-contract quote; long contracts are usually cheaper.
          • Closed models are price-only. Their breakeven throughput is exact arithmetic, but nothing here says what throughput they actually reach or on which hardware.
          • Revenue is not margin. The spread leaves out CPU hosts, networking, storage, staff, idle peak capacity and the API marketplace's fee.

          What would most improve v1

          • Production throughput from providers' own disclosures, or OpenRouter's measured throughput per endpoint.
          • Matched benchmark configurations across GPUs, plus a measured 3:1 sequence.
          • Rental index history and term prices, to backfill the time series and compare on-demand with reserved capacity.
          • Which hardware each provider runs, so each provider's spread uses its real GPU.
          • A small model (8B class) and the newest MoE models once H100 benchmarks exist for them.
